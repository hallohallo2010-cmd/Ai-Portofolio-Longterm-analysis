What am I looking at

Each row further down is one company, and one specific quarter of its earnings that has not been reported yet. A statistical model was asked, in advance, to guess how that quarter will turn out, and its guess was written down and date-stamped before the company filed anything.

The thing being guessed is narrow and worth stating exactly: will the company's diluted earnings per share for that quarter come in higher than for the same quarter one year earlier? Just higher or not higher. It is not a forecast of the share price, of whether the company will beat what analysts expected, or of whether the stock is worth buying.

Nobody knows the answers yet, including the people who built this. The companies have not filed. The results get graded once they do — most report between November 2026 and February 2027, so the earliest a full grading can happen is January 2027. Until then every number here is a prediction with no outcome attached.

These predictions are unscored

No row below has been graded. Any accuracy figure anywhere on this page comes from a different and already-completed test on historical data from 2022–2025 — it is background on how the model has behaved before, not a result for these predictions.

What the probability means

A probability of 0.80 next to a company does not mean the model knows something about that company. It means: among past quarters whose earnings history looked broadly like this one, about 80 in 100 turned out to be beats. It is a base rate borrowed from similar-looking history, applied to a new case. The model has never heard of the company, its products, its management, or anything that happened this quarter.

That matters because the borrowed rate is not equally trustworthy across the range. On the historical test, the model's confident predictions were too confident — and the more confident it got, the worse the gap became.

How often has it been right

This is the historical test: 2022–2025, on data the model had never seen when it was locked. The comparison that matters is not "is it better than a coin flip" — most companies grow earnings most of the time, so always guessing "beat" is already a strong strategy. The bar is beating that.
